How this works

Five tabs, one habit. Start holds the lessons (read them in week 1). Notes is the interactive fingering chart with sound. Songs plays along with you and shows the fingerings as they go by. Tuner listens through the microphone, gives you reference tones and checks your recordings. Plan is your 30-day course with a timer for every step and a practice log.

Everything here follows Yamaha's own "Let's Play Venova!" guidebook, with a saxophone teacher's habits added on top. Where a fingering is marked approx. it is a semitone or a top note: a starting point to confirm against the printed chart in your box and the tuner.

1Meet your Venova
  • What it is: a single-reed instrument (like a saxophone or clarinet) with recorder-style holes. The small side loop near the top, the "branch pipe", is what makes it sound like a sax instead of a recorder.
  • Key of C, two octaves: from C4 (middle C on a piano) up to C6. It is a non-transposing instrument: a written C sounds as a C, so piano, flute and recorder music works as is.
  • Mouthpiece and reed: a soprano-saxophone mouthpiece (equivalent to a Yamaha 4C) with Yamaha's synthetic reed, roughly a strength 2.5. Any soprano sax reed fits.
  • Fingering system: shipped in the German system (a small adaptor sits in the right-middle hole). Remove it and the chart changes to Baroque. This app shows German by default; switch in Notes if you removed the adaptor.
  • Keys: only a few. The octave lever on the back (left thumb) is used only for notes above high D. Two pairs of black keys sit under the right ring finger and right little finger for the lowest notes and some semitones.
  • Care: the ABS body, mouthpiece and synthetic reed can all be washed with water.
2Set up the reed
  1. Mouthpiece on. Twist it gently side to side as you slide it onto the neck. Push it on about two thirds of the way; this position also sets your tuning later.
  2. Ligature on. Slide the metal ring over the mouthpiece from the tapered (tip) end, screws facing you, loose.
  3. Reed in. Flat side against the flat "table" of the mouthpiece, thick end down. Slide the reed up until you can just see a hair-thin edge of the mouthpiece tip above the reed tip. Centre it left to right.
  4. Ligature in place. Centre it so the Yamaha tuning-fork logo sits in its open window (about a centimetre below the reed's shaved part), then tighten the two screws only until the reed stops moving. Over-tightening chokes the sound.
  5. Cap on whenever you put it down. The reed tip is the most fragile part you own.
Teacher's notes. The synthetic reed needs no soaking; a cane reed does (a minute in the mouth or in water). If the first sounds are a struggle after a week, try a cane soprano-sax reed of strength 2 or 2.5: slightly softer reeds speak more easily. Later, Yamaha suggests moving to a slightly stiffer reed once your air is strong, because stiffer reeds help the high notes.

Reed check: never touch the tip, wipe it dry after playing, and store it flat. A chipped or warped reed squeaks no matter what you do.

3Hold it like a player
  • Posture: sit or stand tall, shoulders loose, chin slightly in. The instrument angles down and away from you, roughly 30–45 degrees, so the reed meets the lower lip squarely.
  • Left hand on top: thumb covers the back hole; index, middle and ring fingers cover the top three holes. The thumb also works the octave lever later, by rolling up onto it without leaving the hole.
  • Right hand below: thumb under the thumb hook (it carries the weight), index, middle and ring fingers on the lower three holes, little finger floating just above its pair of black keys.
  • Pads, not tips. Cover each hole with the soft ball of the finger, flat and relaxed. A hole that is 95% covered is 0% covered: it squeaks or jumps up.
  • Keep fingers close. Lifted fingers hover about one finger-width above their holes. Big movements are slow movements.

Yamaha's first note is G: thumb plus three left-hand fingers, right hand doing nothing but holding the hook. Low notes need more skill, so everything in week 1 lives in the left hand.

4First sound and embouchure
  1. Top teeth on the mouthpiece, about 1 cm from the tip. They rest there firmly; the mouthpiece should feel anchored.
  2. Lower lip over the lower teeth, a thin cushion, not rolled in deep. The reed sits on that cushion.
  3. Close the corners of the mouth around the mouthpiece so no air escapes. Inside, shape the mouth as if saying "OH". Chin flat and pointed down.
  4. Breathe deep, blow firmly. The Venova wants more air than a recorder. Start with no holes covered: any sound is a success.
  5. Then G. Four counts, steady. Rest. Six times. Do not bite: pressure comes from the top teeth and firm corners, never from the jaw.

5Air and tongue
  • Breathe low. Put a hand on your stomach: breathing in should push it out, not lift the shoulders. Breathe in through the corners of the mouth without moving the embouchure.
  • Blow steady, blow warm. Imagine fogging a window: a warm, steady, generous stream. Low notes want slower, bigger air; high notes want faster, more focused air.
  • Tonguing. Yamaha's description: touch the tip of the reed with the tip of the tongue, then release. Say "too" (or "doo" for softer starts). The air is already pressing; the tongue just lets it go.
  • Start with the tongue, end with the air. Never close the throat to stop a note. Stop the air and the note stops cleanly.
  • Slur means no tongue between notes: keep blowing while the fingers change. Staccato ("tut") means short and separated. You will practise both in week 3.
Daily long tones are the secret of every good wind player: one note, 8 counts, perfectly level in pitch and volume. Two minutes a day changes everything.
6Music basics in five minutes

Seven names, repeating. Notes are named C D E F G A B, then C again one octave higher: the same note, doubled in frequency, sounding "the same but higher". The number after a letter (C4, C5) says which octave. Tap a key below to hear it and see how the Venova fingers it.

The staff. Five lines; higher on the page is higher in pitch. The lines of the treble staff are E G B D F ("Every Good Boy Deserves Fruit") and the spaces spell F A C E. This app teaches with coloured letter tiles so you can play on day one, and shows the staff beside each note so you absorb reading as you go.

  • Sharps and flats are the notes between: C♯ (also called D♭) sits between C and D. On the Venova they are the hardest notes, which is why the course leaves them for week 4.
  • Other instruments: a piano, flute or recorder plays the same written notes as your Venova. A saxophone or clarinet is a transposing instrument: its written C sounds as a different note, so to play along with a sax player you would each read different music. Your Venova reads the piano's part.
  • Tuning: musicians agree that the A above middle C vibrates 440 times a second (A4 = 440 Hz). The Tuner tab lets you adjust that if you play with a group that tunes differently.
7Rhythm: beats and note lengths

The beat is the steady pulse you tap your foot to. Tempo is its speed in beats per minute (BPM); 60 BPM is one beat per second. Most of the songs here are in 4/4: count "1 2 3 4" and repeat. Amazing Grace and Happy Birthday are in 3/4: "1 2 3".

In the Songs tab, tile width shows length: a whole-note tile is four times as wide as a quarter-note tile. Eighth notes are counted "1 & 2 &".

9Care and tuning the instrument
  • After every session: pull the weighted swab through the body, wipe the mouthpiece and reed dry, cap on. Water inside gurgles and clogs the tiny octave vent (if a high note refuses, blow the moisture out of the branch pipe while pressing the lever).
  • Weekly: the body and mouthpiece can be washed in lukewarm water with a drop of mild soap. Never hot water, never a dishwasher.
  • Tuning: warm the instrument up for a few minutes first (cold plastic plays flat). If everything is flat, push the mouthpiece further onto the neck; if everything is sharp, pull it out a little. Then fix single notes with your embouchure. Stiffer reeds tend to play sharp.
  • Reed life: the synthetic reed lasts months. Replace it when it stops responding or the tip is damaged. Yamaha's replacement is the same reed that came in the box.
10How to practise (and Yamaha's videos)
  • Little and often. 15–20 minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday. Your lip muscles are new; stop when they get wobbly.
  • Always the same shape: warm-up long tones, one new thing, one song, one minute of reflection. The Plan tab is built exactly this way, with a timer per step.
  • Slow is fast. Set the play-along slower than feels necessary and loop it. Speed arrives on its own.
  • Record yourself once a week (Tuner → Check a recording). Your ears improve faster than your fingers, and the recordings prove the progress on bad days.
  • Use the drone and the live tuner. Playing a note against a reference tone teaches intonation better than any needle; the needle tells you which way to move.

    Tune the instrument, then the note

    1. Warm up for 3–5 minutes. A cold Venova plays flat and then creeps up.
    2. Play a long A4 or G4 with the live tuner. Aim for the green zone; small wobbles are normal.
    3. Everything flat? Push the mouthpiece further onto the neck, a millimetre at a time. Everything sharp? Pull it out a little, and check you are not biting.
    4. Fix single notes with the embouchure: a slightly firmer, faster air stream raises the pitch a touch; a relaxed jaw and more "OH" inside the mouth lowers it.
    5. The Venova's semitones (F♯, G♯, B♭) run sharp by design. Yamaha's advice: adjust them with fingering, airflow and embouchure.
